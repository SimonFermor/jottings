Some notes on a webinar _When Grief Keeps You Awake_, an interview of [Neal Walia](https://www.uclahealth.org/providers/neal-walia)(UCLA Santa Monica Pulmonary Sleep Clinic) by [David Kessler](https://grief.com/about-david-kessler/)(grief.com).

Grief will often have an impact on sleep.  The body needs to be in a state of rest and digest but grief can put the body into fight and flight response, with adverse effects on sleep.

How we respond to small threats through the day determines our ability to sleep.  Combined with grief, the adverse impact on sleep can be heightened.

Rumination in the middle of the night when in grief is not unusual.  If this happens it's important to not stay in bed trying to sleep.  If after 20 minutes sleep is not possible, get up and read in another room.  Some people view the middle of the night time when they can't get back to sleep as a good time to do something else.  Seeing this time in a positive way helps to prevent anxiety about sleep from adding to sleeplessness.

Getting out of bed and reading in another room prevents development of bad patterns of behavior.  Reading material should be relaxing and not over-stimulating.

There are three factors that combine to create a sleep disorder:

1. Predisposition (such as habits, conditions, environment)
2. Precipitating Pattern (such as grief)
3. Perpetuating Pattern (poor sleep habits)

Precipitating events can be many years ago.

Wearables can be useful for measuring total sleep, but are not reliable for different stages of sleep.  Too much monitoring of wearable sleep data can be stressful and counterproductive.

It's important to have a wind down time for about an hour before bed time.  Dimming lights and reducing stimuli is helpful.  Relaxing activities and taking a bath can also be helpful.

Medications should be used with caution and not as a first treatment.

The bedroom temperature should be colder than daytime living room temperatures.  A room temperature between 62°F and 68°F is best.  Temperatures above 73° will have adversely impact sleep.

Depression and anxiety are often contributors to sleep disorders.

Anti-histamines can help with sleep onset problems.  Anti-histamines are not suitable for the elderly.

The need for sleep builds up through the day, leading to sleepiness at bedtime.  Napping during the day can reduce sleepiness at bedtime if the nap is too long.  Naps after 2pm are more likely to reduce sleepiness.

Exercise is important in helping the body be relaxed for sleep, but should not be too close to bedtime.

Progressive muscle relaxation is helpful when trying to get to sleep.
